## 1. Alberta/Canadian child-welfare custody & case-worker terminology

### The process and its legal instruments

Alberta's child protection framework operates under the **Child, Youth and Family Enhancement Act (CYFEA)**, RSA 2000, c C-12 (primary text confirmed directly: [Alberta King's Printer PDF](https://kings-printer.alberta.ca/documents/Acts/C12.pdf), retrieved and read in full for this research).

**Ministry name — correction to a commonly-used but now-outdated term:** the government body administering CYFEA is currently named **"Children and Family Services"**, not "Children's Services." Alberta.ca's own ministry page is titled "Children and Family Services" ([alberta.ca/children-and-family-services](https://www.alberta.ca/children-and-family-services), fetched directly), and Searle Turton was sworn in as **Minister of Children and Family Services** in June 2023 ([alberta.ca/minister-of-children-and-family-services](https://www.alberta.ca/minister-of-children-and-family-services); [Wikipedia — Searle Turton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Searle_Turton)), confirming the rename has been in effect since at least mid-2023. The older name **"Children's Services"** persists in some still-live Government of Alberta material (e.g., a 2023-26 ministry business plan titled "Children's Services," and the informal phrase "Children's Services offices" used on some pages), so both names will be found in circulation, but **"Children and Family Services" is the current official name** and is the one this document and any later NextGen artifact should use going forward. Services are delivered directly and through **19 Delegated First Nations Agencies** ([alberta.ca/how-child-intervention-works](https://www.alberta.ca/how-child-intervention-works)).

When a family's situation deteriorates (abuse, neglect, addiction) to the point a court removes a child from parental care, the mechanism is (confirmed via direct fetch of [LawNow: A Brief Overview of Child Intervention Services in Alberta](https://www.lawnow.org/a-brief-overview-of-child-intervention-services-in-alberta/), quoted below):

- **Apprehension** — a Director or peace officer may apprehend a child without a prior order if there are reasonable and probable grounds the child's life/health is seriously and imminently endangered. The Director must then return the child within **two days** or apply to court for one of: a **Supervision Order**, a **Temporary Guardianship Order (TGO)**, or a **Permanent Guardianship Order (PGO)**. The first court date must occur "no more than 10 days after apprehension," with notice served at least two days prior; the guardian(s) and any child aged 12+ must be notified.
- **Supervision Order** — granted when "mandatory supervision of the child and guardian(s) is required to ensure compliance"; the child remains in the guardian's care, for a maximum of six months.
- **Temporary Guardianship Order (TGO)** — granted when the child's needs "cannot adequately be protected while in the guardian's care, BUT it can be anticipated that the child may be returned to the guardian's care within a reasonable time." Granted "for a specified period of time" — a child cannot remain under a TGO indefinitely; maximum limits apply (also corroborated by [Calgary Child Protection Lawyers — TGO](https://familylawyerab.com/areas-of-practice/child-protection/temporary-guardianship-orders-tgo/)).
- **Permanent Guardianship Order (PGO)** — granted when the court is satisfied the child cannot return to the guardian's care "within a reasonable time"; under a PGO **"the Director becomes the sole guardian of the child."** A PGO can later be terminated by the Director's own application or a former guardian's successful application, under specific conditions.
- **Custody Agreement** — a guardian may voluntarily agree to give the Director custody (not guardianship) of the child, placed outside the guardian's care, for a maximum of six months, short of a court order; the agreement must set out a care plan and access arrangements.
- **Kinship care** — where possible, the ministry prioritizes placing a child with extended family/community ("kinship caregivers") before non-family foster care, adoption, or private guardianship ([2017 Kinship Care Handbook, Government of Alberta](https://open.alberta.ca/dataset/90207e69-b458-48e9-b0c5-e24ec896172b/resource/f8e3c712-3d60-41ac-9567-e1889112dc95/download/kinship-care-handbook.pdf) — description drawn from search-result summary of this document, not independently fetched; [alberta.ca/how-child-intervention-works](https://www.alberta.ca/how-child-intervention-works), fetched directly).
- In the large majority of cases (Alberta's own material cites roughly 90%), intervention never reaches removal at all — the ministry works with the family in-home via a safety plan, and the **parent retains full legal guardianship** throughout ([alberta.ca/how-child-intervention-works](https://www.alberta.ca/how-child-intervention-works)).

The primary statute itself directly confirms the TGO/PGO/Custody Agreement terminology: s.128.1(2) refers to "a child who is the subject of a temporary guardianship order or a permanent guardianship order or agreement, or a youth who is the subject of a custody agreement" (Act text, read directly from the King's Printer PDF).

### The employee's title

Alberta.ca's own public-facing description of the program (fetched directly) uses **"caseworker"** as the generic term for the employee who works with the family/child ([alberta.ca/how-child-intervention-works](https://www.alberta.ca/how-child-intervention-works)). Government of Alberta job postings for this role use the formal title **"Child Intervention Caseworker"** (also seen as "Caseworker – Child Intervention"), with related tiers **"Child Intervention Practitioner"** and **"Child Intervention Supervisor"** ([Government of Alberta job postings, accessed 2026-09-21 — job-board URLs are not stable long-term, treat as corroborating rather than primary](https://ca.linkedin.com/jobs/view/child-intervention-caseworker-and-assessor-at-government-of-alberta-2924359405); [jobpostings.alberta.ca — Child Intervention Supervisor](https://jobpostings.alberta.ca/job/High-Level-Child-Intervention-Supervisor-High-Level/596484917/)).

**Correction from an earlier draft of this research:** the CYFEA statute itself does **not** use the word "caseworker" anywhere. Having read the full Act text directly (King's Printer PDF, converted and text-searched), the confidentiality provision at **s.126** speaks only in terms of **"the Minister," "the Director,"** and **"any person employed or assisting in the administration of this Act, including an agency providing services on behalf of a director."** The exact text of s.126(1): "The Minister and any person employed or assisting in the administration of this Act, including an agency providing services on behalf of a director, may disclose or communicate personal information that comes to the Minister's or person's or agency's attention under this Act only in accordance with the **Protection of Privacy Act**, in proceedings under this Act, in accordance with Part 2, Division 2 or this Part or as follows: (a) to any person or organization, including an agency providing services to a child, if the disclosure is necessary to plan services for or provide services to the child or the child's family or to plan or provide for the day-to-day care or education of the child; (b) to the guardian of the child ... or the guardian's lawyer; (c) to the child ... or the child's lawyer; (d) to any person employed in the administration of child protection legislation in another province or territory of Canada; (e) to any person with the written consent of the Minister." Notably, s.126(1)'s current text (as amended by **AR 141/2025**) already cross-references the **Protection of Privacy Act** by name — direct primary-source confirmation that the 2025 FOIP → ATIA/POPA split (discussed in Section 3 below) is reflected in CYFEA's own current text, not just in commentary about the reform. Section 126(4) also confirms the HIA-custodian interaction directly: "A custodian may disclose health information to a director or a person acting on behalf of a director ... for the purposes set out in subsection (3)" (assessment/investigation/service provision) — meaning the Act contemplates a "director" (the legally designated decision-maker under s.129) and "a person acting on behalf of a director, including an agency providing services on behalf of a director" as the operative legal categories, not "caseworker."

**Conclusion on terminology:** "caseworker" is the correct **practice/job-title** term — the one used in public-facing government communication and in actual job postings — for the person who, in practice, acts on the Director's behalf under CYFEA. But the **statute's own legal category is "the Director" (and "a person/agency acting on behalf of a director")**, not "caseworker." NextGen's client-facing/glossary usage of "Case Worker" is well-supported as the practical, plain-language term to use (matching how Alberta.ca itself talks to the public); it just should not be represented as the Act's own defined term if this workspace ever needs to cite the statute directly. "Guardianship worker" was not found as a current standard title in any source consulted — "caseworker" (one word, sometimes "case worker" as two) is what's actually in use.

### How custody status changes who decides

- **Parent/guardian retains legal guardianship** (no order, or only a Supervision Order in place): the parent/guardian remains the decision-making authority; the caseworker's role is supportive/monitoring, not substitutive.
- **Custody Agreement or Temporary Guardianship Order**: the Director (via the assigned caseworker) has custody/guardianship for the order's duration, but the arrangement is explicitly framed as time-limited with an expectation of eventual return to the original guardian.
- **Permanent Guardianship Order**: the Director is the child's **sole guardian**; the caseworker acting on the Director's behalf is the only party with legal authority to make decisions in place of a parent.

This confirms the client-call resolution already logged as **OQ-03 (RESOLVED)**: "Case Manager," "Case Worker," and "legal guardian" are the same external relationship-role in different custody circumstances, and the client's chosen standard term, **"Case Worker,"** aligns with both the public-facing "caseworker" term the ministry (Children and Family Services) itself uses and the formal job title "Child Intervention Caseworker." This research adds a nuance not previously in the register: **the Case Worker relationship is not uniform** — a portal's data model may eventually need to distinguish a caseworker acting under a TGO (temporary, time-boxed authority) from one acting under a PGO (sole permanent guardian, standing in for the parent entirely) from a caseworker in a purely supportive/monitoring role where the parent retains guardianship — because the scope of what that Case Worker can authorize for the child (e.g., consenting to treatment, receiving records) genuinely differs by order type. This is a nuance for the permission-matrix and entity-model work (items 4–5), not a resolved rule.

**Needs client/counsel confirmation:**
- Whether NextGen's portal needs to actually record *which* order type (Supervision / Custody Agreement / TGO / PGO) applies to a given client, or whether it's sufficient to record only "Parent/Guardian" contact vs. "Case Worker" contact without further granularity. This is a scope call, not something public sources can answer.
- Whether NextGen ever deals with Delegated First Nations Agency caseworkers under different title conventions than the provincial ministry's — worth asking the client directly if any referrals come through a DFNA rather than the provincial ministry (Children and Family Services) directly.
- Any nuance specific to consent-for-treatment or consent-for-disclosure authority split between a PGO caseworker and a still-involved parent (e.g., cultural/kinship consultation rights) should be confirmed with the client's own legal counsel, since it touches CYFEA sections beyond what this research reviewed in full.

## 2. Government/community-issued client identifiers (referral IDs)

No public source located during this research documents a single standardized "file number" or "case number" format issued by Alberta Children and Family Services (or FSCD, PDD, AISH, or the Jordan's Principle program) that is published for external/public reference. This is expected — these are internal case-management identifiers, not public-facing account numbers, so their format isn't documented anywhere a non-employee could find it. What the research does establish:

- **Alberta Children and Family Services** (see Section 1 above for the current-name correction) operates child intervention casework across 5 regions and 19 Delegated First Nations Agencies ([alberta.ca/how-child-intervention-works](https://www.alberta.ca/how-child-intervention-works)). Referral/reporting to the ministry is described in public material ([alberta.ca — Child protection referral form](https://www.alberta.ca/child-protection-other-professional-referral-form); [Reporting a Concern, Government of Alberta, 2018](https://www.humanservices.alberta.ca/documents/reporting-a-concern.pdf)), but no internal case-numbering scheme is published in any source surfaced.
- **FSCD** (Family Support for Children with Disabilities) and **PDD** (Persons with Developmental Disabilities) are distinct Alberta programs with separate eligibility/application processes — FSCD for under-18s, PDD/AISH for adults ([Entrust Disability Services — how to apply](https://www.entrustdisabilityservices.ca/blog/how-to-apply-for-disability-alberta/)) — implying each almost certainly issues its own internal file identifier tied to its own case-management system, but no format detail is public.
- **Jordan's Principle**, administered federally by Indigenous Services Canada (not a provincial body), has its own separate request/application intake ([sac-isc.gc.ca — Submit a request under Jordan's Principle](https://www.sac-isc.gc.ca/eng/1568396296543/1582657596387)), again with no published case-number format.
- No evidence was found that any of these bodies use a shared or cross-referenceable identifier format with each other, or that community agencies making referrals are routinely handed a portable, standardized ID they pass along to a receiving agency like NextGen.

**What this means for the "Client ID" field decision:** the practical implication is that a referral's originating ID is realistically **heterogeneous by source** — different format, different presence/absence, different internal meaning depending on whether the referral comes from Children and Family Services, FSCD, PDD, AISH, Jordan's Principle, a Delegated First Nations Agency, or a self-/family-referral with no external ID at all. Nothing in the public record supports treating "Client ID" as one standardized field with a predictable format; it behaves more like a **free-text external reference** whose meaning depends on which "Referral Source" field it's paired with. This is consistent with, and adds detail to, the already-resolved **OQ-13**: the register's note that "format TBD by research, likely optional" is corroborated — this research did not surface a discoverable standard format, and did not find evidence intake staff would reliably have one to record at all for every client. Whether the entity model needs one generic "External Referral ID" field (paired with a "Referral Source" field to give it context) or several source-specific fields is exactly the design question item 9 (the entity model) still needs to decide — this research leans toward the former being more realistic, since the sources vary too much to standardize sub-fields per agency, but that is a modelling judgment call for the entity-model author, not a finding this research can settle on its own.

**Needs client/counsel confirmation:**
- Direct confirmation from NextGen intake staff (not inferable from public sources) on which referral sources *actually* hand over a written ID at intake in practice, versus which almost never do.
- Whether any specific referring body NextGen deals with regularly (their contact at Children and Family Services, a specific DFNA, etc.) has told them a definite format for that body's ID — this is exactly the kind of practical, non-public detail only the client's own referral-intake experience can supply.

## 3. Applicable Canadian/Alberta privacy, health-information, and Indigenous data-sovereignty framework

### PIPEDA (federal)

The federal **Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act** generally applies to organizations' collection/use/disclosure of personal information "in the course of commercial activity." Non-profit status is **not** an automatic exemption — commercial activity is the operative test, not incorporation type ([OPC — How PIPEDA applies to charitable and non-profit organizations](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-laws-in-canada/the-personal-information-protection-and-electronic-documents-act-pipeda/r_o_p/02_05_d_19/); [CBA — Why charities and not-for-profits should comply with PIPEDA](https://www.cba.org/Sections/Charities-and-Not-for-Profit-Law/Articles/2019/comply-with-PIPEDA)). Critically, organizations subject to a **"substantially similar"** provincial private-sector law (Alberta's PIPA is one of these, along with BC's and Quebec's) are generally **exempt from PIPEDA** for information handled within that province ([OPC — Q&A on PIPEDA/PIPA application](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-laws-in-canada/the-personal-information-protection-and-electronic-documents-act-pipeda/r_o_p/02_05_d_26/)). Practical read: for an Alberta-only non-profit like NextGen, **PIPA is the operative statute in practice**, with PIPEDA as a backstop for anything crossing provincial/international lines.

### Alberta PIPA vs. the former FOIP / new ATIA + POPA

**PIPA (Personal Information Protection Act)** governs private-sector organizations, and it applies to non-profits **conditionally**: some non-profit structures (e.g., trade unions, condo boards, school councils, churches) are subject to PIPA in full by virtue of how they're incorporated; other non-profits are subject to PIPA **only for their commercial activities** — with accepting charitable donations explicitly *not* counted as commercial activity ([alberta.ca — Personal information for non-profits and other organizations](https://www.alberta.ca/personal-information-for-non-profits-and-other-organizations); [OIPC PIPA Guide 2008](https://www.oipc.ab.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/PIPA-Guide-2008.pdf)). **This is the key open determination for NextGen**: whether its incorporation structure puts it in the "fully covered" bucket or the "commercial-activity-only" bucket is a fact about NextGen's own corporate structure, not something public search can resolve.

Alberta's public-body legislation has recently changed: the **Freedom of Information and Protection of Privacy Act (FOIP)** was repealed effective **June 11, 2025**, and split into two new statutes — the **Access to Information Act (ATIA)** and the **Protection of Privacy Act (POPA)** — governing roughly 1,200 Alberta public bodies ([BLG — Alberta overhauls its public sector access and privacy regime](https://www.blg.com/en/insights/2025/01/alberta-overhauls-its-public-sector-access-and-privacy-regime); [Gowling WLG — Alberta overhauls public-sector privacy and access to information law](https://gowlingwlg.com/en/insights-resources/articles/2025/alberta-overhauls-public-sector-information-law); [Brownlee LLP — FOIP – No More!](https://news.brownleelaw.com/post/102kgkj/foip-no-more-albertas-new-public-sector-access-to-information-and-privacy-leg)). This matters for terminology accuracy going forward in this workspace: **"FOIP" is no longer the current name of the public-body regime** as of mid-2025; the current names are ATIA and POPA. NextGen, as a non-profit society rather than a government department, school board, municipality, health authority, or other designated public body, is **not** a "public body" under this regime and would not be subject to ATIA/POPA — PIPA (private-sector law) is the applicable regime instead, provided NextGen is not itself specifically designated a public body by regulation (no evidence found that it would be).

### Alberta Health Information Act (HIA) and medication administration records

HIA governs **"custodians"** — a defined, closed list of health-sector entities (regional health authorities/AHS, licensed pharmacies, nursing homes, ambulance operators, licensed primary-care providers, and other entities specifically designated in the Health Information Regulation) — plus their **"affiliates"** (employees, volunteers, contractors, and agencies under contract to a custodian). *Sourcing caveat:* direct fetches of the OIPC's HIA overview page failed repeatedly during this research (connection errors on both attempts); this description is drawn from search-engine summaries of that page and of [U of A — Am I a Custodian or an Affiliate?](https://www.ualberta.ca/en/medicine/resources/faculty-and-staff/informatics/get-hip/custodians-or-affiliates.html), not a directly verified read of the primary source or the Health Information Regulation itself. Treat the custodian/affiliate description below as directionally reliable but **not independently confirmed against the regulation's actual text** — this is exactly the kind of statutory-list question counsel should verify directly rather than relying on this research. A community non-profit providing residential/group-home support and administering medication is **not obviously on the custodian list** as described in public sources — the custodian list is built around licensed health-service providers (pharmacies, health authorities, licensed care facilities) rather than social-service agencies. No source found explicitly designates a group-home/child-and-family social-service non-profit as an HIA custodian. This means **NextGen's Medication Administration Records are more likely governed by PIPA (as "personal information," potentially including "personal health information" as a PIPA subcategory) than by HIA** — but this is an inference from what HIA's custodian list is built to cover, not a confirmed determination, since the full Health Information Regulation custodian list was not fully enumerated in the sources retrieved.

### CYFEA confidentiality provisions

Read directly from the Act text (King's Printer PDF, s.126, quoted in full above): the confidentiality obligation runs against **"the Minister and any person employed or assisting in the administration of this Act, including an agency providing services on behalf of a director"** — not specifically "the Director" alone, and not "caseworker." Disclosure is permitted only in accordance with the Protection of Privacy Act, in CYFEA proceedings, or under a defined list of exceptions: (a) to any person/organization (including a service-providing agency) where necessary to plan or provide services or day-to-day care/education for the child; (b) to the child's guardian or the guardian's lawyer; (c) to the child or the child's lawyer; (d) to a person administering child-protection legislation elsewhere in Canada; (e) to anyone with the Minister's written consent. None of this is framed as mandatory disclosure — it is a list of *permitted* disclosures, not a duty to disclose to any of these parties. A reporter's identity is separately protected as privileged under s.126.1 and may not be disclosed in evidence (per statute text; not independently re-verified in this pass beyond the earlier search summary of s.126.1's existence). **Relevance to NextGen:** s.126 governs what the *ministry* (Children and Family Services) must/may keep confidential and disclose about its own case file — it constrains what a Case Worker can share with NextGen and what NextGen might expect to receive under s.126(1)(a) (an agency providing services to the child). It does **not** appear to directly regulate NextGen's own records about a client — NextGen is the "agency providing services" contemplated by the Act, not the Minister/Director, so NextGen's own confidentiality obligations toward the same information would run through **PIPA**, not CYFEA directly. This distinction is worth flagging clearly since it's easy to conflate "the government's file is confidential under CYFEA" with "our file about the same client is governed by CYFEA" — the latter does not follow from the former on this research.

### Breach notification (PIPA)

PIPA breach notification to the OIPC is **mandatory** wherever "a reasonable person would consider there exists a real risk of significant harm" (the **RROSH** test) — notification is required "without unreasonable delay," and even a single affected individual triggers the obligation ([OIPC — Breach Notification Requirements](https://oipc.ab.ca/breach-notification/); [OIPC — Guidance for Notifying the OIPC about a Privacy Breach under PIPA, April 2024](https://oipc.ab.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Guidance-for-Notifying-the-OIPC-about-a-Privacy-Breach-Under-PIPA-April-2024.pdf)). Alberta was the first Canadian jurisdiction to have mandatory private-sector breach reporting ([OIPC — PIPA amendments](https://oipc.ab.ca/pipa-amendments-2/)). Practical implication for the portal: an audit-trail/disclosure-log capability (already anticipated in item 7's scope) is not just good practice but supports meeting this notification obligation if it's ever triggered.

### Canadian data residency

There is **no single blanket Canadian law requiring all personal data to stay in Canada**; residency requirements instead come from (a) specific government funding/contract clauses that mandate Canadian hosting, and (b) the general PIPA/PIPEDA requirement that when data is processed outside Canada (or by an offshore processor), the organization remains responsible for equivalent safeguards and must be transparent about it ([Pilotcore — Canadian Data Residency: Cloud Rules, PIPEDA, and Risk](https://pilotcore.io/blog/canadian-data-residency-and-the-public-cloud); [ITeam — Canadian Data Residency: What You Need to Know](https://theiteam.ca/news/email-encryption-systems-for-canadian-data-residency-requirements/)). Since NextGen is funded through government contracts/programs, it is plausible (though not confirmed by this research) that specific funder agreements impose a Canadian-hosting requirement — this is a contract-terms question, not a general-law question, and needs to be checked against NextGen's actual funding agreements.

### OCAP and Indigenous data sovereignty — scope caveat

**OCAP® (Ownership, Control, Access, Possession)** is specifically a **First Nations** data-governance framework developed and stewarded by the **First Nations Information Governance Centre (FNIGC)**; it asserts that First Nations communities collectively own and control data about themselves and set their own protocols for access ([FNIGC — The First Nations Principles of OCAP®](https://fnigc.ca/ocap-training/); [Wikipedia — First Nations principles of OCAP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Nations_principles_of_OCAP)). Alberta First Nations leadership formally endorsed OCAP via a 2010 Assembly of Treaty Chiefs resolution. **Important scope nuance for NextGen, whose clients are described more broadly as "Indigenous and marginalized"**: OCAP is not automatically the governance framework for Métis or Inuit clients. Métis data governance is a **separate and still-emerging** effort — the Métis Nation has only recently begun articulating its own principles (e.g., the *Métis Health Research and Data Governance Principles*), distinct from and not identical to OCAP ([Oxford Academic — Promoting self-determined Indigenous data governance in Canada: the Métis Health Research and Data Governance Principles](https://academic.oup.com/heapro/article/41/1/daaf229/8424030)). Alberta also has 8 **Métis Settlements** with their own governance relationship to the province ([alberta.ca/metis-settlements](https://www.alberta.ca/metis-settlements)). **Practical implication**: a single blanket "we follow OCAP" statement in NextGen's cross-cutting concerns would overstate what OCAP actually covers — it is not a universal Indigenous data framework, it's First-Nations-specific, and applying its principles to Métis or Inuit clients as if by default would need to be a NextGen policy choice made deliberately (in consultation with those communities), not something inferable from the OCAP framework itself extending automatically. This connects directly to the register's existing **OQ-15** (whether Indigenous community/Nation consultation on data handling has actually happened) — this research doesn't resolve OQ-15, but it does add that if/when that consultation happens, "which framework for which clients" is itself a live question, not just "has consultation happened."

**Needs client/counsel confirmation (all of section 3):**
- Whether NextGen's specific incorporation structure places it in PIPA's fully-covered bucket or the commercial-activity-only bucket — a corporate-structure fact only NextGen's own counsel can confirm.
- Whether NextGen or any of its programs/contracts (e.g., data submitted to Alberta Health Services, involvement with Alberta Health Services' Netcare, or a licensed nursing/medical component) actually makes it an HIA "affiliate" of some custodian — this research found no clear designation either way and it needs a direct legal read of the Health Information Regulation's full custodian list against NextGen's actual service delivery model.
- Whether NextGen's specific funding agreements (provincial contracts, FSCD/PDD/Jordan's Principle-linked funding, etc.) contain explicit Canadian-data-residency clauses — this is a contract-review question, not a general-law question.
- The retention-period question already flagged as **OQ-14** remains genuinely unresolved by this research — no public source gives a NextGen-specific mandated retention period; the "7 years post-discharge" figure in the discovery doc was already flagged there as needing confirmation against NextGen's actual licensing/funding/contractual obligations, and this research did not surface anything that settles it.
- Whether and how to apply Indigenous data-sovereignty principles to Métis and Inuit clients (not just First Nations clients covered by OCAP) is a policy/consultation question for NextGen and the communities it serves, not something this research or any public source can settle.
- General note: none of the above should be read as legal advice; a lawyer engaged by NextGen should confirm applicability and obligations before any compliance design decision is finalized.
